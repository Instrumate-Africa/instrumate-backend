# COMBINATIONS OF SPEECH AND SIGNING (SIMULTANEOUS COMMUNICATION)

Some of the simultaneous communication approaches are:

## (a) Speech with Supporting Signs

Here, speech is accompanied and supported by a few signs, finger-spelling, gestures, pointing and mime. The aim of this approach is
to supply the deaf person with some additional visual information in order to help him in the ambiguous lip-reading and thus in the
understanding of the spoken language.

## (b) Signing Exact English

Signing Exact English is an invented communication system in which each individual spoken word in the correct English sentence is followed
by one or more signs. The signs used are taken partially from American sign language (ASL) and partially consist of newly invented signs
plus ‘‘signs’’ for affixes, suffixes, plural and other grammatical devices. It is believed that by use of this communication system,
English spoken language will be visualized, and thus made easier for the deaf person to see and learn. However, this system has been found to
be slow and very ambiguous, as it violates both the ‘‘pure’’ spoken language as well as the signs from the sign language. This happens in
much the same way as when one spoken language is literally translated word for word into another spoken language, thus breaking the rules
of their individual grammar, distorting meaning and twisting logic as in the examples below:

e.g.

Kiswahili:                           Nyumbani kwenu ni wapi?
English word for word translation:   House-in yours is where?
— for                                Where is your home?

English:                             How many children do you have?
Kiswahili word for word translation: Vipi nyingi watoto wafanya wewe una?
— for                                Una watoto wangapi?

## (c) Pidgin Sign Language
This is a communication approach that has developed naturally when the hearing and deaf people are together. Through a mutual knowledge,
respect, and understanding of each other's language, it has been possible to use a communication approach in which the hearing person
continues to speak his mother-tongue, but now accompanied by the signs for quite a big number of words as well as finger-spelling.

At the same time, the hearing person applies certain crucial visual/grammatical rules such as proper use of space, use of classifiers,
inflection of verbs and use of non-manual articulators when communicating with a deaf person. The speed of speaking with signing is at
a natural rate, and the message carried out in a visually understandable grammar, leading both auditorily dependent hearing impaired
and visually dependent hearing impaired (deaf) to benefit from this communication.

The deaf person on the other adds voice to his communication and incorporates certain important grammatical rules from the spoken language.
These include some grammatical markers and a specific word order in an attempt to enhance understanding.

As pointed out earlier, the signs have to be followed by the non-manual characteristics such as body movements, facial expressions,
eye and/or mouth movements, in order to gain their full linguistic meaning. As words in a spoken language are put together
like ‘‘pearls on a string’, following a strict set of grammatical rules, so are signs put together in ‘‘Columns of ex- pressions’ — with
simultaneous execution of both the manual and non-manual aspects of the signs — also governed by equally strict grammatical rules.

These rules, however, can be quite different from some of the rules of a spoken language.
