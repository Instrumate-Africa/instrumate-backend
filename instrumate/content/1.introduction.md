# INTRODUCTION

HISTORICALLY, most deaf communities have been restricted in certain aspects of human development by being encouraged to communicate
in aural/oral languages of the hearing society only. This restriction has left the deaf handicapped in their ability to communicate with hearing
as well as with other deaf people.

In an attempt to communicate much more easily, deaf communities the world over have, over the years, developed their own
national visual/gestural sign languages, which have provided not only an efficient and effective vehicle for communication,
but also for thought and learning.

Sign languages, however, have been severly suppressed by hearing societies who have culturally not been able to appreciate their value.
They have also been suppressed by some post-lingual and not culturally deaf people. A widespread misconception has been that sign languages
are crude collections of gestrures used in an unsystematic manner by the deaf, for only very basic and concrete communication.

These misconceptions have been strongly dispelled by renowned researches over the past twenty years. An increasing number of scientific studies
have shown that different national sign langugages are highly developed, heavily inflected and structured and as valid for communication,
learning and human development as any spoken language.

Deaf people in Kenya, as in most other countries, have lived in the country's unique national culturre from which their specific, national
language has evolved.

This dictionary is a first attempt to records some of the signs that are but a small part of the KSL vocabulary. This selection of signs
will no doubt be subject to some criticism as local variations of the vocabulary exists and KSL is still developing very rapidly.
